### The problem

A surgeon operating on something they cannot see directly works from a screen: a
fluoroscopy feed, or a camera view on a monitor across the room. Every glance at it is a
glance away from the patient, and the mapping between what is on the screen and what is
under the hands has to be held in the surgeon's head.

SAAR removes the screen. It projects the patient's own anatomy, reconstructed from their
scans, onto the surface of the patient, so the thing being looked at and the thing
being operated on are in the same place.

### How it works

A registration pipeline turns preoperative CT into a 3D reconstruction and lines that
model up with the body on the table. A calibrated projector, carried on a motorised
frame, casts it onto the surgical site.

Bodies move during a procedure, so a fixed projection would drift out of register within
minutes. A splotch-based tracking algorithm watches the site and drives the frame to
keep the overlay sitting where it should.

Two networks add to what is projected. A U-Net convolutional network segments the
preoperative scans and marks internal abnormalities, and those regions are highlighted
in the projection rather than left for the surgeon to find. A second network, a
CNN-LSTM, predicts how organs in the chest will move. An LSTM is built to work on
sequences, which is what breathing and a heartbeat are, so the overlay can follow the
anatomy in real time instead of showing where it was when the scan was taken.

### Where it stands

The models were trained and validated on large public CT and MRI datasets, and the
accuracy of the projection alignment was measured separately. The case being made is as
much economic as clinical: a projector and a frame against the cost of existing
image-guidance systems.

This description is drawn from the project abstract. The fuller write-up, with per-model
accuracy, alignment error and the registration maths, is not yet published here.
